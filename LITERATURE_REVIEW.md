# Literature review and next experiments

This project studies a specific model: three identical firms sell one homogeneous product, face a linear inverse demand curve `P_t = A_t - b Q_t`, have constant marginal cost `c`, and update quantities simultaneously by moving 55% toward best responses to last month's rival quantities. `A_t` is synthetic and forecast before decisions; firms currently maximize expected profit. A fixed-demand local stability result is not a claim that a changing, stochastic market converges to a fixed equilibrium.

## Demand volatility

Robert Engle, *GARCH 101: An Introduction to the Use of ARCH/GARCH Models in Applied Econometrics* (provided PDF, pp. 5-9, 15-18), explains how ARCH and GARCH model predictable changes in the **conditional variance** of errors, separate from forecasting the conditional mean. The GARCH(1,1) recursion uses the last squared shock and last forecast variance; squared standardized residuals are a diagnostic. His application is to financial returns and risk. Our generator instead uses a fixed innovation standard deviation and an AR(1) shock, so adding GARCH to the current mean forecast has no theoretical justification by itself. A sensible extension is to generate a documented variance-regime or clustered-volatility scenario, test squared training residuals for ARCH effects, then compare out-of-sample interval coverage and decision outcomes. With 72 training months, fit and calibration uncertainty would be substantial.

## Cournot adjustment and stability

Cars H. Hommes, Marius I. Ochea, and Jan Tuinstra (2018), *Evolutionary Competition Between Adjustment Processes in Cournot Oligopoly: Instability and Complex Dynamics*, *Dynamic Games and Applications* 8, 822-843, especially pp. 826-829 and 831-836. Their general short-memory adjustment framework explicitly includes partial adjustment toward a best reply. Increasing the number of firms can destabilize simple synchronous response rules; an endogenous mix of Nash and adaptive firms can stabilize locally while producing complex dynamics elsewhere. Our model has a fixed three-firm population and a single fixed adjustment rate; it does not implement evolution between decision rules. Compare several adjustment speeds and firm counts, checking the linearized eigenvalues and observed paths, before proposing evolutionary switching.

Akio Matsumoto and Ferenc Szidarovszky (2014), *Theocharis Problem Reconsidered in Differentiated Oligopoly*, discussion paper 218, especially pp. 2, 4-11, 18. They derive distinct conditions for positive outputs and stability when goods are differentiated, and show that discrete versus continuous adjustment matters. Our firms sell the **same** good, so their differentiated-products thresholds do not directly apply. Their key methodological lesson does: positivity of an equilibrium, whether an actual best response hits the nonnegative boundary, and stability of an adjustment process are different statements.

Zhengyang Liu, Haolin Lu, Liang Shan, and Zihe Wang (2025), *On the Oscillations in Cournot Games with Best Response Strategies*, arXiv:2410.09435v3, especially pp. 1-3, 5-6, 14. For fixed linear demand with nonnegative quantity constraints and simultaneous full best responses, the authors characterize convergence to equilibrium or a two-period oscillation, including heterogeneous costs. Their conclusion also states that their oscillation theorem extends to weighted-average updates. That statement should not be read as a result for our forecast-driven, time-varying market. Use their nonnegative-boundary treatment and compare undamped versus damped fixed-demand trajectories; do not label changing-demand tracking errors as endogenous two-cycles.

For our symmetric interior fixed-demand map, the common-mode eigenvalue is `1 - alpha*(n+1)/2` and the difference-mode eigenvalue is `1 - alpha/2`. At `n=3, alpha=0.55` these are `-0.1` and `0.725`, so the Nash point is locally stable. At `n=3, alpha=1`, the common eigenvalue is `-1` and convergence cannot be asserted from a strict-stability test. The current fixed-demand convergence result is therefore consistent with these papers. The former `all_periods_interior` check only tested `A_t>c`; the updated simulation separately checks whether each **unclipped best response** is positive at each decision.

## Risk aversion and uncertain demand

Marcus Asplund, *Risk-averse firms in oligopoly*, SSE/EFI working paper 69, revised February 2000 (provided PDF, especially pp. 1-2, 6-10, 18-19; later published in 2002). The direction of the effect of risk aversion depends on whether firms set prices or quantities and on the source of uncertainty. Under uncertain demand and quantity choice, increased risk aversion lowers the firm's desired output. The working paper analyzes much wider preferences and conditions than this project; its results should not be assumed for arbitrary shocks or to mean that risk-averse firms always produce less in every market.

Jim Jin and Shinji Kobayashi (2023), *Monopoly profit lower than oligopoly due to risk aversion*, *Economics Bulletin* 43(2), 1010-1015 (provided PDF, pp. 2-5). They model a linear inverse-demand intercept shock and symmetric Cournot firms maximizing expected profit minus `delta` times profit variance. For conditional demand variance `sigma²`, this gives the risk-aware best reply `max(0, [E(A)-c-b Q_rivals]/[2(b+delta sigma²)])` and symmetric quantity `max(0, [E(A)-c]/[(n+1)b+2 delta sigma²])`. At `delta=0` this reduces to our existing model. Under the paper's assumptions, sufficiently strong risk aversion and demand variance can reverse the usual monopoly versus oligopoly expected-profit ranking. This project has neither risk aversion nor a monopoly experiment, so that reversal is **not** a finding of the existing simulation. Their analysis assumes an appropriately bounded demand shock; our Gaussian generator has unbounded support, another reason to label any numerical extension as a new model with its own assumptions.

## Recommended sequence

1. Compare SARIMAX, seasonal naive, and perfect-information decision forecasts on **the same generated demand paths and initial quantities**. Report forecast error, realized profit, and distance to the realized-demand Nash benchmark. Perfect information is an oracle bound, not an available policy. This directly tests whether the better mean forecast helps firms.
2. Vary `alpha` and `n` under fixed demand first, then under moving demand. Distinguish local fixed-point stability, clipped responses, and the ability to track a changing benchmark. Hold demand draws paired across comparisons.
3. Add a separate mean-variance risk-aware scenario using a forecast available at decision time for both `E(A_t)` and conditional variance; compare quantities and expected profit with risk-neutral firms. Report risk-adjusted utility separately from realized profits. The risk-aware Nash benchmark needs the new denominator above; the existing risk-neutral benchmark is no longer appropriate for that scenario.
4. Introduce clustered volatility and consider GARCH only if a residual diagnostic and out-of-sample comparison warrant it. Do not fit a variance model solely because the demand level varies over time.

These six sources motivate extensions and clarify the current assumptions. None invalidates the existing `alpha=0.55` fixed-demand eigenvalues or removes the two reporting/validation bugs corrected alongside this review. The earlier `initial_distance` measured the distance after the first update; it now records the actual starting state and separately reports the distance after one update.
